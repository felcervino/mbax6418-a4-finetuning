# S10 Report

**What I compared.** Three ways of labeling 300 Amazon Gift Cards reviews (100 per class) as POSITIVE, NEUTRAL or NEGATIVE, using star ratings as the answer key (4 or 5 stars positive, 3 neutral, 1 or 2 negative). The first two use a large hosted model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (35 billion parameters, about 3 billion active at a time): once with no examples and once shown 6 examples. The third is a small model I trained myself, Gemma 3 4B (4,329,881,968 parameters, of which 29,802,496 were adjusted through a small add-on called an adapter, on a compressed 4-bit copy of the model, using Unsloth). Training ran on a Tesla T4 GPU, tested directly, for 114 steps over 2 passes through 900 training reviews, and took 678.8 seconds (about 11 minutes).

**Why these choices.** The small model had to fit a T4; Gemma 3 4B used 5.15 GB during training. Training settings follow Unsloth's documentation (rank 16, learning rate 0.0002, 16 reviews per step, seed 3407). The 6 examples are training reviews, the first 2 per class from a shortlist built by a fixed rule (150 to 450 characters, no "Five Stars" titles). Two are noisy: review 13547 has 4 stars but describes a confusing reload, and 9272 has 1 star but reads like a neutral question. No test review was used.

**Results** (300 test reviews, no invalid answers). Balanced accuracy is the average share of each class found correctly; macro-F1 is a similar average that also penalizes wrong guesses.

| Approach | Balanced accuracy | Macro-F1 | Correct / incorrect | NEUTRAL recall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hosted, no examples | 0.747 | 0.717 | 224 / 76 | 0.35 |
| Hosted, 6 examples | 0.747 | 0.718 | 224 / 76 | 0.35 |
| Fine-tuned Gemma 3 4B | 0.810 | 0.805 | 243 / 57 | 0.63 |

**Best approach, and where it fails.** The fine-tuned model scored highest, mostly on NEUTRAL reviews: the hosted model called 50 (no examples) or 51 (6 examples) of 100 neutral reviews negative, the fine-tuned model 23. Review 30030 ("Ok but I won't do it again", 3 stars) was called negative by both hosted runs and neutral by the fine-tuned model. Its weakness is the other direction: it called 11 of 100 negative reviews neutral, against 1 for the hosted model with no examples, for example review 30137 ("Not Amazons Usual Excellence", 1 star). All three missed review 30008 (a 3-star pop-up complaint, called negative) and review 30011 (title and text both "Gift", 5 stars, called neutral).

**A caution about titles.** 50 reviews have titles like "Five Stars", which give the rating away. The fine-tuned model got all 50 right (the hosted runs 39 and 42). In a check I ran afterwards, outside the notebook (evidence/star_title_sensitivity.txt), its lead shrinks on the other 250 reviews: 193 correct, against 185 and 182.

**What the statistics support.** A McNemar test counts only reviews where two approaches disagree. The fine-tuned model was right alone on 34 reviews against 15 for the hosted model with no examples (p = 0.0094), and on 31 against 12 versus 6 examples (p = 0.0054). The two hosted runs split 6 and 6 (p = 1.0), so the examples changed nothing measurable. A small p-value only says the disagreements lean one way on these 300 reviews. Without the star-word titles the gap is no longer clear (p = 0.26 and p = 0.09). Limits: one dataset, one small model, one set of 6 examples, one run each; the hosted model is far larger; star ratings are a noisy answer key, especially at 3 stars. In the final run, 238 of 300 hosted no-example answers came from an earlier attempt that stopped on a file error.

**Is the gain worth the cost?** Training took 678.8 seconds on one T4, produced a 110 MB adapter, and needed 900 labeled reviews. Labeling the 300 test reviews then took 141.6 seconds on that T4 using 6.23 GB. The hosted model needs a remote service. The fine-tuned model scored 0.063 higher in balanced accuracy, but much of that lead is in the star-word titles, so I consider the gain modest and not firm.

**A decision I investigated with my agent.** My first small model, Phi-4-mini, trained only 8,912,896 parameters. The agent showed this was exactly 2 of the model's 4 kinds of layers, because Unsloth did not recognize its combined layer names, and a retest gave the same result. It offered three options (keep the weaker adapter, work around Unsloth, switch models) and recommended Gemma 3 4B. I made the choice: the switch (option A) over keeping the weaker two-layer adapter (B) or working around Unsloth (C). B would have evaluated an adapter weaker than the one I approved, C meant custom code outside Unsloth's supported path, and A had a documented Unsloth recipe for Gemma 3 4B (DECISIONS.md D-05 re-decided). A new check, which fails unless all 7 kinds of layers are trained, passed with Gemma 3 4B (238 trained modules) and again after reloading the saved adapter in a fresh runtime. One consequence: the adapter file names Unsloth's 4-bit copy (unsloth/gemma-3-4b-it-unsloth-bnb-4bit) as its base, because the original google/gemma-3-4b-it requires access approval.
